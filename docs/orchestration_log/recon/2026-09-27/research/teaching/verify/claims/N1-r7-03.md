# N1-r7-03 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
In 4 lab studies, bottom-quartile performers on humor/grammar/logic tests (12th percentile actual) rated themselves at the 62nd percentile; miscalibration is linked to a metacognitive-skill deficit, and training that raised participants' actual skill also improved their ability to recognize their own limitations — the least competent are the least able to self-assess accurately, exactly where a trainer would most need an honest self-report signal. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.77.6.1121 (Kruger & Dunning, 1999).

## Primary text (quote, location)
Full text is closed access (APA PsycArticles paywall; no OA route via Unpaywall/Semantic Scholar — this DOI was previously logged in an N4 survey round as having "no abstract available," but the APA PsycNet abstract page is in fact readable):

> "Across 4 studies, the authors found that participants scoring in the bottom quartile on tests of humor, grammar, and logic grossly overestimated their test performance and ability. Although their test scores put them in the 12th percentile, they estimated themselves to be in the 62nd. Several analyses linked this miscalibration to deficits in metacognitive skill, or the capacity to distinguish accuracy from error. Paradoxically, improving the skills of the participants, and thus increasing their metacognitive competence, helped them recognize the limitations of their abilities."

Every specific element of the claim (4 studies, 12th vs. 62nd percentile, metacognitive-deficit link, training-improves-recognition finding) matches this abstract verbatim.

## Replications & critics

A Crossref bibliographic search ("Kruger Dunning unskilled unaware reanalysis") surfaces an active critique/reply literature around this exact paper: Krueger & Mueller (2002, 10.1037/0022-3514.82.2.180) argued the effect is partly a regression-to-the-mean/better-than-average-heuristic artifact; Kruger & Dunning replied directly (10.1037/0022-3514.82.2.189, "Unskilled and unaware — but why? A reply to Krueger and Mueller"); Ackerman et al. (10.1016/j.joep.2013.07.004) offer a "signal extraction" counter-explanation; Ehrlinger et al. (10.1016/j.obhdp.2007.05.002) extend the original authors' own work. None of these were read in full for this verification (out of scope — this claim cites only the original 1999 paper), but their existence means the specific numeric claim (12th vs. 62nd percentile) sits inside a live methodological dispute about *why* the effect occurs, not whether the numbers themselves are disputed.
