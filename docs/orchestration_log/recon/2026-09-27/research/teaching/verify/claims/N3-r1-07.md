# N3-r1-07 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed

"An intervention that gives gaming students supplemental exercises targeting exactly the material they bypassed, plus negative-affect messaging from an animated agent, reduces gaming behavior; students who received many supplemental exercises learned considerably better than gaming students in the control condition." DOI 10.1007/11774303_39. Surveyed S3 R1 O1, secondary source (WebSearch synthesis) quoting the abstract.

## Primary text (quote, location)

Baker, Corbett, Koedinger, Evenson, Roll, Wagner, Naim, Raspat, Baker, Beck, "Adapting to When Students Game an Intelligent Tutoring System," Proceedings of ITS 2006, LNCS 4053, pp. 392-401. Full text read: author-affiliated PDF at `pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Baker_2006.pdf` (Unpaywall/Semantic Scholar both report `CLOSED`/no OA location; PDF located via author's CMU PACT-lab publication mirror).

Abstract, verbatim (matches surveyed quote exactly): "Students using this system engage in less gaming, and students who receive many supplemental exercises have considerably better learning than is associated with gaming in the control condition or prior studies."

Results section, gaming reduction: "33% of students were seen gaming in the control condition, while 18% of students were seen gaming in the experimental condition, a marginally significant difference, χ2(1,N=102)=3.30, p=0.07."

Results section, learning: "The third of students that received the most supplementary exercises had significantly better learning than the other two thirds, t(37)=2.25, p=0.03." Table 1: experimental-condition high-exercise group gained 46 points vs. 20 points for the control-condition high-harmful-gaming group, t(47)=2.09, p=0.04.

Results section, mechanism decomposition (not in the abstract, materially qualifies it):
- "there was not an overall improvement in learning... t(70)=0.34, p=0.73" (whole-sample comparison, before subgroup analysis).
- Exercises did not track with reduced individual gaming: "the students who received more exercises gamed significantly more often than the students who received fewer exercises, χ2(1,N=388)=24.33, p<0.001" — the opposite direction from a "exercises reduce gaming" reading.
- Anger expressions alone: "Students who received more expressions of anger did not have a significantly larger average learning gain than other students... t(37)=0.48, p=0.63, effect size=0.20σ" and no relationship to gaming reduction over time (p=0.84, p=0.36).

## Design facts

| field | value |
|---|---|
| design | Quasi-experiment, single-cohort, both conditions in sequence (not condition-order-randomized): all students did control condition first, experimental second; a scheduling confound forced dropping one of two planned lessons (percents) from analysis, leaving only the scatterplot lesson |
| n | 51 experimental / 51 control assigned; 39 experimental / 34 control usable for learning-gain analysis after pre/post-test absences excluded |
| population | middle-school mathematics students, 5 classes, 2 schools, Pittsburgh suburbs (K-12) |
| instrument | pre/post-test, items counterbalanced, identical to past studies using the same tutor lesson |
| delay-to-test | immediate — post-test given right after an 80-minute tutor-use block across multiple class periods in the same study, not a ≥7-day-delayed or transfer measure |

## Grade (S R O, changed from survey? why)

S=2, **changed from surveyed S=3**: this is not a clean RCT — condition order was not randomized (control always first), a scheduling confound eliminated half the planned design, and the intervention's own headline gaming-reduction effect is "marginally significant" (p=0.07) by the paper's own wording, not significant at the conventional 0.05 threshold. R=1 (K-12 population), unchanged. O=1 (immediate post-test, same study session), unchanged.

## Status: VERIFIED

The abstract sentence is confirmed verbatim against the primary text, and the specific learning-gain comparison it rests on (46 vs. 20 points, p=0.04) is a real, adequately-powered result in the paper. But the claim compresses two mechanistically separate findings into one causal story that the primary source itself does not support at that resolution: the paper's own decomposition shows the anger/negative-affect component had no measurable effect on either gaming or learning by itself, and that supplemental exercises correlated with *more*, not less, individual gaming (p<0.001) — so "plus negative-affect messaging... reduces gaming" describes the paper's overall marginally-significant (p=0.07) finding, not an effect either component produced independently.
