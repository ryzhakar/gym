# N6-r2-13 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
A quantitative synthesis of 317 spacing-effect experiments finds the retention benefit of distributed practice depends on the ratio between inter-study lag and the length of the retention interval being tested, not on absolute spacing length — meaning an "irregular bursts toward daily" schedule's effect on durability is in principle a ratio to be tuned, not merely a regularity to be maximized, though the underlying studies are lab verbal-recall tasks rather than CS-adjacent skill.

Full text was read there (publisher-mirror PDF, verified against Crossref metadata). ## Text match check
N2-r1-01's confirmed quotes (abstract, p.354; body, "Discussion of absolute-lag effects"):
> "Analyses suggest that ISI and retention interval operate jointly to affect final-test retention; specifically, the ISI producing maximal retention increased as retention interval increased."
> "Moreover, the optimal ISI increased as retention interval increased. For instance, at retention intervals of less than 1 min, ISIs of less than 1 min maximized retention; at retention intervals of 6 months or more, ISIs of at least 1 month maximized retention."

N2-r1-01 additionally confirms the "317 experiments" figure against the design table (n = 839 assessments, 317 experiments, 184 articles) and the paper's own "ratio" framing ("optimal ratio of ISI to retention interval"), and confirms the population as lab-based verbal-memory tasks, not a professional/CS-adjacent skill (R=1 in that verification).

Match: "317 spacing-effect experiments," the ISI:retention-interval ratio finding (not absolute spacing length), and "lab verbal-recall tasks rather than CS-adjacent skill" all match the confirmed source directly. The claim's "irregular bursts toward daily" phrase is the synthesizer's own scheduling-design language, applying the paper's ratio finding to a hypothetical schedule — not a quote from the source, and correctly not presented as one.
