# N4-r6-05 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
In a randomized trial with 69 general-practice residents diagnosing progressively harder cases, feedback on prior diagnoses did not improve residents' diagnostic-confidence calibration for the more difficult cases (no significant difference in absolute accuracy or bias between feedback and no-feedback groups), and a post-hoc analysis found feedback made calibration on cases the resident diagnosed CORRECTLY slightly worse, plausibly because seeing their mistakes on other cases lowered their confidence even where it should not have. DOI 10.1007/s10459-021-10080-9.

## Primary text (quote, location)
Full text read (Europe PMC open-access copy). Methods (statistical-analysis paragraph) and Results:

> "The analysis of calibration on all 12 cases, showed no effect of condition on absolute accuracy, F (1, 67) = 1.64, p = 0.204, ηp2 = 0.02 or bias F (1, 67) = 1.87, p = 0.176, ηp2 = 0.03. The mean bias in the whole sample (M = 0.18) significantly differed from zero, t (68) = 7.22, p < 0.001, and thus showed that on average, participants were slightly but significantly overconfident."

> "The exploratory analysis (Table 4) of incorrect cases only ... showed no effect of condition, F (1, 67) = 0.19, p = 0.665, ηp2 < 0.01. The exploratory analysis of correct cases only, which would indicate the degree of underconfidence, showed a significant effect of condition, F (1, 67) = 6.55, p = 0.013, ηp2 = 0.09, with the feedback condition being more underconfident (M = − 0.35) than the no-feedback condition (M = − 0.25)."

Discussion:

> "Against expectations, feedback did not improve diagnostic calibration when compared to the control condition without feedback. Exploratory analyses even showed that the feedback made participants significantly more underconfident on correctly diagnosed cases than participants in the control condition ... this may have made them more cautious on subsequent cases, resulting in lower confidence ratings regardless of their actual performance."

Every element of the claim — the two null main-effect p-values (.204, .176), the significant post-hoc worsening on correctly diagnosed cases (p=.013, feedback more underconfident), and the "seeing mistakes lowered confidence even where it shouldn't have" mechanism the paper itself proposes — matches the primary text verbatim.

## Design facts (table)
| field | value |
|---|---|
| design | RCT, 2-condition between-subjects (feedback vs. no-feedback), 12 cases per resident |
| n | 69 general-practice residents (Netherlands) |
| population | general-practice medical residents — adult professionals, one-to-one/self-paced coached hard-skill population |
| instrument | diagnostic accuracy (rated by 2 GPs, ICC=0.96) vs. self-reported confidence, transformed to a common scale; absolute-accuracy and bias calibration measures |
| delay-to-test | immediate — confidence/accuracy rated case-by-case within the same session, across 12 cases |

## Replications & critics
OpenAlex citation-graph search unavailable (daily budget exhausted). The paper itself situates its null/negative result against a contrasting prior finding — Nederhand et al. (2018), which found feedback *did* improve calibration, but on easier cases — and offers this as the likely explanation for the discrepancy (case difficulty), rather than a contradiction. A Crossref bibliographic search for related calibration-feedback work surfaces "Calibration Feedback with the Practical Scoring Rule Does Not Improve Calibration of Confidence" (10.1002/ffo2.199 / preprint 10.31234/osf.io/dw89q) — a different study, not a replication of this one, but reporting a convergent null-feedback-effect pattern in a related calibration-research context.
