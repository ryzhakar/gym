# N5-r6-03 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
Surgery residents coached on the expert's quiet-eye behavior (not just hand movements) improved knot-tying performance more than residents given traditional technical instruction over an equal, short training period (mean improvement QET=28% vs TT=17%). DOI 10.1016/j.surg.2014.05.004 (Causer, Vickers, Snelgrove, Arsenault & Harvey, 2014, *Surgery*).

## Primary text (quote, location)
Full text read (Unpaywall open-access copy). Abstract/Results:

> "Both groups improved their knot tying performance (p < 0.05) from pretest to the low anxiety conditions (mean difference: QET = 28%; TT = 17%), however, only the QET group maintained their knot tying performance in the high anxiety conditions..."

Results section (body, not just abstract):

> "The ANOVA revealed a group by condition interaction (F(4,72) = 2.78, p = 0.03, ηp2 = 0.13; see Figure 2). As predicted, both the QET and TT groups significantly increased their knot tying performance from pretest to the low anxiety conditions (p < 0.05). While the QET group maintained performance in the high anxiety conditions, the TT group performance decreased to pretest levels."

The 28%/17% figures and the interaction statistic both confirm the claim.

## Design facts (table)
| field | value |
|---|---|
| design | RCT, random assignment to QET (quiet-eye training) vs. TT (traditional technical training) arms; group × anxiety-condition mixed ANOVA |
| n | 20 first-year surgery residents (~age 26) |
| population | adult first-year surgery residents |
| instrument | knot-tying performance score (low-anxiety and high-anxiety test conditions) |
| delay-to-test | 10 minutes after training (immediate) |
