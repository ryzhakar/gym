# N6-r4-05 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
In a UK panel of 18,053 adults, a stronger pro-environmental attitude predicted lower probability of commuting by car, but only among people who had recently moved home: at 12 months since moving, predicted car-use probability was .55 (strong attitudes) vs .63 (weak attitudes); by 10 years since moving the gap had nearly closed (.61 vs .66). The authors read this as a temporary "window of opportunity" opened by a context break that narrows faster than the ~12-month period assumed in prior work. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0153490.

## Primary text (quote, location)
Full text read (PLOS ONE, open access PDF from `journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0153490&type=printable`). Results, thesis-internal p.10 of the article:

> "Again holding covariates constant at their mean, after living in the same address for 12 months, people with low environmental attitudes have a predicted probability of .63, and those with strong attitudes have a predicted probability of .55 for commuting by car. ... Living at the same address for 10 years, the predicted probability of commuting by car for people with low environmental attitudes is .66, compared with predicted probability of commuting by car for people with high environmental attitudes of .61, and these differences become increasingly smaller as the time spent living at the same location increases."

Discussion, near p.11 ("The declining influence of contextual changes"):

> "The original proposal by Verplanken et al. [15], suggested that a period of 12 months may be a suitable period to evaluate the habit discontinuity effect... However, we find that the strongest differences in predicted probability were observed in the very early months after moving home and soon decreased over time, and so our results suggest delivering resources as soon as possible to capitalise on this natural break in context."

All four predicted-probability figures (.63/.55 at 12 months; .66/.61 at 10 years) match the claim exactly. The "window narrows faster than the 12-month period assumed in prior work" framing matches the discussion's stated departure from Verplanken et al.'s 12-month assumption.
