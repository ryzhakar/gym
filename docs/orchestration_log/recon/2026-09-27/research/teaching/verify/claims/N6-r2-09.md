## Claim as surveyed
A preregistered two-sample study (pilot n=251, replication n=372) introducing the Adult Hyperfocus Questionnaire finds hyperfocus is not exclusive to ADHD but is significantly more prevalent and more frequent, across school, hobbies, and screen-time settings, in adults with higher ADHD symptomology — establishing hyperfocus as a measurable, replicated trait, though the study measures prevalence only, not any channeling intervention.

Abstract (NCBI PubMed record, verbatim, matches ledger's own quote_location):
> "Adults with ADHD often report episodes of long-lasting, highly focused attention, a surprising report given their tendency to be distracted by irrelevant information. This has been colloquially termed 'hyperfocus' (HF). Here, we introduce a novel assessment tool, the 'Adult Hyperfocus Questionnaire' and test the preregistered a priori hypothesis that HF is more prevalent in individuals with high levels of ADHD symptomology. We assess (1) a pilot sample (n = 251) and (2) a replication sample (n = 372) of adults with or without ADHD. Participants completed highly validated scales, including the Conners' Adult ADHD Rating Scale, to index ADHD symptomology. Those with higher ADHD symptomology reported higher total and dispositional HF and more frequent HF across each of the three settings (school, hobbies, and screen time) as well as on a fourth subscale describing real-world HF scenarios."

Quote matches claim's numbers exactly: pilot n=251, replication n=372, three named settings (school/hobbies/screen time). The claim's "not exclusive to ADHD" is an inference, not a verbatim abstract phrase — the abstract frames HF as continuously scaled with ADHD symptomology across a sample "with or without ADHD," consistent with (but not stated as) "not exclusive to ADHD." The claim's "measures prevalence only, not any channeling intervention" is an accurate characterization: the abstract describes assessment/correlation, no intervention.
