# N3-r6-11 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
Backward-fading worked-example practice (last steps solved first) paired with feedback that showed a similar worked-out solution produced the highest near- and far-transfer scores among the studied combinations. DOI 10.1002/j.2168-9830.2009.tb01007.x.

## Primary text (quote, location)
Same source and same access limitation as N3-r6-02 (Wiley paywall; `cache.py` exhausted every route and failed). Crossref abstract:

> "First, students who practiced by solving all problem steps and those who practiced by solving a gradually increasing number of steps starting with the first step first (forward-fading practice) produced higher near-transfer scores than those who were asked to solve a gradually increasing number of steps but starting with the last step first (backward-fading practice) ... Finally, students who learned with backward-fading practice produced higher near- and far-transfer scores when feedback included the solution of a similar worked-out problem."

**Partial gap found on this read:** the abstract's first finding states backward-fading alone (without the specific feedback type) produces *lower* near-transfer scores than forward-fading or full-step practice. The third finding is a narrower, within-backward-fading comparison: backward-fading *with similar-worked-example feedback* beats backward-fading with other feedback types. The abstract does not itself rank this specific combination against every other combination in the study (e.g., forward-fading + similar-example feedback), so the claim's framing — "the highest ... among the studied combinations" — is a reasonable but not abstract-confirmed superlative; it may be true, but the abstract only supports a narrower, within-condition comparison.
