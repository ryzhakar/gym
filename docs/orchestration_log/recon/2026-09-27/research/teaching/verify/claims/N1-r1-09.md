# N1-r1-09 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
A meta-analysis of 317 spacing experiments finds the training-schedule spacing that maximizes retention itself depends on the delay-to-test used to measure it, so a single fixed test delay cannot serve as a schedule-independent durability instrument.

## Primary text (quote, location)
Same source as N2-r1-01: Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), "Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall Tasks: A Review and Quantitative Synthesis," *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), 354–380. DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354. Full text read from the same PDF already fetched for N2-r1-01 (https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Cepeda2006.pdf; escholarship.org's own copy still 403s via CloudFront, both the item page and direct PDF URL — tried again for this claim, same result).

Abstract (p.354), count confirmed:
> "This review found 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments located in 184 articles... Analyses suggest that ISI and retention interval operate jointly to affect final-test retention; specifically, the ISI producing maximal retention increased as retention interval increased."

Summary section (p.371), the sentence that most directly supports the claim's "instrument" framing:
> "The magnitude of the observed distributed practice benefit depends on the joint effects of ISI and retention interval; retention interval influences the peak of this function."

Educational Implications section (p.370–371):
> "It is unfortunate that we cannot say with certainty how long the ISI should be to optimize long-term retention... it is unclear how quickly retention drops off when intervals exceed the optimal ISI. If the field of learning and memory is to inform educational practice, what is evidently needed is much less emphasis on convenient single-session studies and much more research with meaningful retention intervals."

The claim's specific wording ("schedule-independent durability instrument") is not the paper's own phrase — it is an accurate paraphrase/inference from these passages: because the optimal ISI shifts with the retention interval used to test it, any single fixed test delay picked to compare spacing schedules will favor whichever schedule happens to be optimal at that one delay, not a schedule-independent verdict. "317 experiments" matches the abstract's count exactly.

## Design facts (table)
(Same source and dataset as N2-r1-01 — repeated here for this claim's own record.)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Design | Meta-analysis / quantitative synthesis of primary spacing-effect studies, with moderator (retention-interval × ISI) breakdowns |
| n | 839 assessments, 317 experiments, 184 articles; ~85% of analyzed data points are from young-adult participants (Table 10) |
| Population | Mostly young adults in lab verbal-memory tasks (list recall, paired-associate); some child studies noted as too sparse at longer retention intervals to draw conclusions |
| Instrument | Final recall/recognition test after a fixed retention interval, compared across ISI conditions; the paper explicitly flags retention interval choice as a methodological variable, not a fixed instrument |
| Delay-to-test | Retention intervals synthesized range from seconds to 30–2,900 days (~8 years) |
