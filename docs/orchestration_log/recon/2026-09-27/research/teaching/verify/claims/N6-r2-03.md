# N6-r2-03 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
In its six-sample founding study (adult general population, Ivy League undergrads, West Point cadets, spelling-bee competitors), grit — perseverance and passion for long-term goals — accounted for only about 4% average variance in success/retention outcomes and did not correlate with IQ, a modest effect size for a trait often invoked to explain sustained practice.

## Primary text (quote, location)
Full text read: Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews & Kelly (2007), "Grit: Perseverance and Passion for Long-Term Goals," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 92(6), 1087–1101. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.92.6.1087. PDF: Wayback Machine mirror of the author's original UPenn page, http://web.archive.org/web/20170703053625/http://www.sas.upenn.edu/~duckwort/images/Grit%20JPSP.pdf (Unpaywall, Semantic Scholar, and PMC all returned closed/no record for this DOI; live UPenn URL 403s; this is the author-hosted copy, archived).

Abstract (p.1087):
> "The authors tested the importance of 1 noncognitive trait: grit. Defined as perseverance and passion for long-term goals, grit accounted for an average of 4% of the variance in success outcomes, including educational attainment among 2 samples of adults (N = 1,545 and N = 690), grade point average among Ivy League undergraduates (N = 138), retention in 2 classes of United States Military Academy, West Point, cadets (N = 1,218 and N = 1,308), and ranking in the National Spelling Bee (N = 175). Grit did not relate positively to IQ but was highly correlated with Big Five Conscientiousness."

Table 3 ("Summary of Major Findings," p.1099) gives the per-study numbers behind the abstract's "average of 4%":
> "Study 1... 4.8a***; Study 2... 4.8a***; Study 3... 6.3**; Study 4... 3.9c***; Study 5... 1.4c*; Study 6... 3.8a,c*" (% variance in success explained by grit), with r between grit and IQ of −.20 (Study 3, SAT), −.05 (Study 4), −.08 (Study 5), .02 (Study 6).

Match, with one nuance: the claim's "did not correlate with IQ" is stronger than the paper's own phrasing. The abstract says grit "did not relate *positively*" to IQ, and Table 3 in fact reports two statistically significant small **negative** correlations (r = −.20, p<.05; r = −.08, p<.01) alongside two near-zero ones — i.e., grit trended slightly negative with IQ where it correlated at all, not simply "no correlation." The claim's summary is a reasonable compression of "no positive relationship" but overstates it as an absence of correlation.

## Design facts (table)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Design | Six independent samples: 2 cross-sectional adult-community surveys, 1 cross-sectional undergraduate survey, 2 longitudinal (3-month) military-cadet cohorts, 1 longitudinal (1-month) youth-competition cohort |
| n | 1,545; 690; 138; 1,218; 1,308; 175 (six samples) |
| Population | Adults aged 25+ (community, 2 samples); Ivy League undergraduates; West Point cadets (young adults, 2 entering classes); National Spelling Bee finalists (predominantly children/adolescents — not adults) |
| Instrument | Self-report Grit Scale (12-item, two facets: Consistency of Interest, Perseverance of Effort); outcome measures were educational attainment, GPA, military retention, and spelling-bee competition round reached |
| Delay-to-test | Cross-sectional for Studies 1–3 (no delay); longitudinal 3 months (cadet retention) and ~1 month (spelling bee) for Studies 4–6 |

## Replications & critics
No OpenAlex/WebSearch (budgets exhausted). Crossref bibliographic search for grit/perseverance/IQ turned up numerous downstream applications (nursing students, migrant children, chronic-pain patients) but no direct comment/reanalysis targeting this 2007 paper itself. The field's standard critical reanalysis of this exact line of work is Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017), DOI 10.1037/pspp0000102 — verified separately as N6-r2-04 in this same batch — which reports a much smaller pooled grit–performance correlation across 66,807 individuals and finds grit's proposed two-facet higher-order structure unconfirmed; noted here as corroborating critical context on a different DOI, not conflated with this claim's own source.

## Grade (S R O, changed from survey? why)
S=3, R=2, O=0 — unchanged from survey. Six large-N samples with stated regression/correlational models but no experimental control (S=3); population mixes adults, undergraduates, military cadets, and children — not adults acquiring CS-adjacent or comparable professional hard skills specifically (R=2); outcome is self-reported trait predicting attainment/retention/GPA, not measured unaided skill performance (O=0) — this claim cannot enter a recommendation set regardless of S.
