# N1-r1-02 — Verification

## Claim as surveyed
"The common 'skill per hour' instrument -- fitting a power-law learning curve to averaged practice-trial data -- is a statistical artifact of averaging; individual learners' practice curves are better fit by an exponential function." (DOI 10.3758/bf03212979)

## Primary text (quote, location)
Full text fetched via Internet Archive Wayback Machine snapshot (2026-07-11) of the Unpaywall-listed Springer PDF (`link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03212979.pdf`) — direct fetch of that URL is blocked by a Springer bot-challenge/login wall; the archived copy is the same publisher PDF.

Abstract (p.185): "The power function is treated as the law relating response time to practice trials. However, the evidence for a power law is flawed, because it is based on averaged data. We report a survey that assessed the form of the practice function for individual learners and learning conditions in paradigms that have shaped theories of skill acquisition. We fit power and exponential functions to 40 sets of data representing 7,910 learning series from 475 subjects in 24 experiments. The exponential function fit better than the power function in all the unaveraged data sets. Averaging produced a bias in favor of the power function."

Quote and numbers match the claim exactly and at the location cited (Abstract).

## Replications & critics
Semantic Scholar citation list (paperId `3b9b728a846035240921c10ce8cf67f8a8946108`, 200 of ~736 citing works scanned by title) surfaced no direct "reanalysis"/"comment" retracting or reversing the core finding (power-law-by-averaging artifact). One relevant boundary-condition critique found and abstract-checked via Crossref:

- "How Common Is the Exponential Decay Pattern of Motor Skill Acquisition? A Brief Investigation" (DOI 10.1123/mc.2020-0043, 2021): re-fitting individual three-parameter exponential-decay curves on a complex functional upper-extremity motor task, finds only 13.3% of young adults, 40.9% of older adults, and 66.7% of adults with Parkinson disease show the "classical" individual exponential pattern — i.e., the individual-exponential generalization does not transfer cleanly to complex motor-skill tasks or heterogeneous populations, even though it does not re-test or challenge Heathcote et al.'s own (simpler RT-task) data sets.

This is a genuine moderator/boundary finding (task complexity, population heterogeneity), not a refutation of the claim as stated for the paradigms Heathcote et al. actually surveyed (memory search, counting, arithmetic — simple RT tasks). No study was found reasserting the power law over the exponential law for these same paradigms. "The Shape of Learning Curves: A Review" (DOI 10.1109/TPAMI.2022.3220744) was found by title only; its content was not fetched (out of scope for a single claim's replication check without further time) and is listed here only as a candidate for the exclusion-register/index tiers to check, not as evidence either way.
