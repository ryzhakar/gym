## Disputes

- **[education]** #4/#6/#26 (Sweller cognitive load line) vs. #7 (Kirschner/Sweller/Clark) together vs. #17 (Kapur, productive failure): direct-instruction-first (worked examples reduce load, minimal guidance fails for novices) versus struggle-before-instruction (productive failure benefits from unsupported initial attempts) — same evidence base (cognitive load, novice working memory), opposite prescriptions; not resolved by either side's cited studies alone. Flagged for Tier 4 per the plan's named standing dispute ("worked examples vs productive failure").
- **[education]** #29 (Kulik & Kulik 1988: immediate feedback wins in applied settings) vs. classic list-learning results the same paper cites as showing delayed feedback wins in verbal-lab tasks: the meta-analysis itself reports the reversal by setting, not a contradiction between two anchors — recorded here because a future searcher will otherwise assume one direction of the "immediate vs. delayed feedback" question is settled.
- **[education]** #14 (Soderstrom & Bjork: performance during training misleads about learning) is the interpretive frame that #21's finding (delayed test-like feedback beats immediate) and #27's field RCT (interleaving costs performance now, pays off on delayed test) both instantiate; not a disagreement, but three anchors making the same underlying point from different data — noted so a synthesizer does not triple-count it as convergent independent evidence.
- **[cs-education]** **Aptitude-by-mental-model-consistency, unsettled.** Bornat's PPIG 2006/2008 claim that a pre-course "mental model consistency" test bimodally predicts CS1 success (the informally circulated "camel has two humps" line of work) has no resolvable DOI — it exists as an ACM DL identifier (`10.5555/1379249.1379253`) that Crossref does not carry, so it does not enter this index as a citation (rule 4). The nearest resolvable, closely related study, #3 (Caspersen, Larsen, Bennedsen 2007), tested a similar mental-model-consistency measure independently and reports results that do not confirm a strong, clean predictive split — an unresolved and only partly citable dispute.
- **[cs-education]** **Parsons problems: effective, but not uniformly.** #5 and #17 report measurable learning/assessment benefits from Parsons problems in their specific study designs; #19, the field's own systematic review of the same literature, reports the effect sizes across studies are inconsistent and moderated by problem design (e.g., "faded" vs. full Parsons problems) — the practice is well-evidenced in aggregate but not as a uniform effect.
- **[cs-education]** **Block-based vs. text-based programming, contested intuition.** The common assumption that block-based environments are a lesser stepping-stone and text-based is the "real" skill being deferred is not supported by #23's quasi-experimental comparison, which found comparable learning outcomes — a case where a course-design intuition and the measured evidence diverge.
- **[coaching]** **Ericsson (1993, 2004, 2019, 2020) vs. Macnamara/Hambrick/Oswald (2014) and Hambrick/Macnamara/Oswald (2020):** whether deliberate practice is the dominant driver of expert performance (Ericsson) or explains only a modest, domain-varying share of variance, smallest in sports and games (Macnamara et al.) — an active, multi-round dispute (#17–25), not resolved in this index.
- **[coaching]** **VanLehn (2011) vs. Baker (2016) and Mackay (2001):** VanLehn's synthesis is read by some as showing human tutors' adaptivity/intelligence drives their advantage over less-adaptive systems; Baker and, earlier, Mackay argue the advantage is substantially explainable by simpler, non-intelligent mechanisms (engagement, pacing, availability) — a dispute over mechanism, not over whether human tutoring works (#28, #33, #34).
- **[ai-tutoring]** #17 (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025 — ungated GPT-4 access harms unassisted exam performance) vs. #16 (Kestin et al., Sci Rep 2025 — a structured AI-tutor protocol outperforms active learning): both are RCTs on AI-assisted learning but disagree on net direction; the plausible moderator is guardrail/structure design, not "AI tutoring" as one undifferentiated treatment.
- **[ai-tutoring]** #24 (Kosmyna et al. 2025 — EEG evidence of "cognitive debt" from ChatGPT-assisted writing) vs. #25 (Stanković et al. 2025 — comment disputing the EEG-connectivity inference and statistical basis of #24): a direct methodological critique pair on the same claim.
- **[ai-tutoring]** #15 (Wu & Yu 2023 meta-analysis — pooled positive effect of AI chatbots on learning outcomes) vs. #17 (Bastani et al. 2025 — a single large RCT finding harm on unassisted outcomes specifically): the meta-analysis pools mostly assisted/immediate outcome measures (its own inclusion criteria are not O≥1 by this project's outcome-match rule), so the apparent disagreement may be an artifact of outcome-type, not a real contradiction — flagged for Tier 3/4, not resolved here.
- **[adult-skill]** **Deliberate practice's explanatory power.** Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993, #21) and Ericsson (2004, 2015, #22/#23) argue deliberate practice is the dominant driver of expert performance, including in medicine. Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014, #24), meta-analyzing the same construct across domains, find deliberate practice explains a moderate share of variance in games and music but a much smaller share in professions — directly disputing the strength (not the existence) of the effect Ericsson's papers claim.
- **[adult-skill]** **Whether CRM training demonstrably works.** Salas et al. (2001, #28) frame CRM evidence as consistently positive at the reaction/learning level. Five years later, O'Connor et al. (2006, #29), reviewing an expanded set of the same kind of studies, reach a more cautious verdict — evidence is still concentrated at reaction/attitude level, with behavior- and organizational-outcome evidence remaining thin — and issue an explicit call for stronger outcome designs, unresolved as of that update.
- **[adult-skill]** **Skill-decay curve and its moderators.** Arthur, Bennett, Stanush & McNelly (1998, #42) established the classic quantitative retention-decay curve (steeper for cognitive vs. physical/procedural tasks, moderated by overlearning and retention interval). Tatel & Ackerman (2025, #43), meta-analyzing procedural skill retention specifically with a much larger and more recent evidence base, revise several of those moderators — flagged here as an update/partial revision rather than a flat contradiction, since #43 narrows scope to procedural skills where #42 was domain-general.
